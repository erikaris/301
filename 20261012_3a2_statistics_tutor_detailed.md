## 1. Context and terminology

Two analyses are considered: correlations between seven corresponding Children's Eating Behaviour Questionnaire (CEBQ) and Adult Eating Behaviour Questionnaire (AEBQ) subscales; and comparisons of CEBQ scores between FHS and non-FHS groups. **FHS is not defined in the supplied question** and must be clarified. Confirm whether observations are paired within families and whether multiple people belong to the same household.

## 2. Normality

A normal distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped. Assess distributional shape using histograms, normal Q–Q plots, skewness, excess kurtosis, and (where useful) the Shapiro–Wilk test. Values of skewness and excess kurtosis between -1 and +1 are informal descriptive guidelines, not proof of normality. Ordinary kurtosis is 3 under normality; **excess** kurtosis is 0.

Shapiro–Wilk tests the null hypothesis that the observations come from a normal population. A small p-value is evidence against exact normality, but does not automatically invalidate a parametric analysis. Conversely, a large p-value does not establish normality.

**Illustrative diagnostics (not generated by the code below):** $n=330$, skewness $=-0.42$, excess kurtosis $=0.35$, Shapiro–Wilk $p=0.012$. Inspect the Q–Q plot and any influential observations, and then check assumptions specific to the intended test.

```r
# Reproducible example; output differs from the illustrative values above
set.seed(123)                       # Make the simulation reproducible
scores <- rnorm(330, 3.2, 0.65)    # Generate hypothetical quantitative scores
hist(scores, breaks = 20)          # Inspect the distribution
qqnorm(scores)                     # Compare observed and normal quantiles
qqline(scores)                     # Add normal-reference line
shapiro.test(scores)               # Test exact population normality
# install.packages("moments")      # Install once if necessary
library(moments)                   # Load descriptive-moments functions
skewness(scores)                   # Calculate sample skewness
kurtosis(scores) - 3               # Calculate excess kurtosis
```

**SPSS:** Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Explore > Plots > Histogram and Normality plots with tests.

## 3. Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

For independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance, the standardised sample mean approaches a standard normal distribution as sample size increases:

$$
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i
$$

$$
\frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).
$$

The standard error of the mean is:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\bar{X})=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.
$$

The CLT concerns the **sampling distribution of a mean**, not the distribution of individual observations. There is no universal $n \gt 30$ guarantee. Inference for Pearson correlation is not justified solely by invoking the CLT.

```r
set.seed(123)                               # Reproducibility
population <- rexp(100000, rate = 1)       # Create a skewed population
sample_means <- function(n) {              # Define a sampling function
  replicate(5000, mean(sample(population, n))) # Repeat sampling and average
}
hist(population, breaks = 60)              # Skewed original observations
hist(sample_means(5))                      # Means of samples of size 5
hist(sample_means(30))                     # Means of samples of size 30
hist(sample_means(100))                    # Means of samples of size 100
```

## 4. Pearson and Spearman correlation

Pearson's $r$ describes **linear** association between quantitative variables; Spearman's $\rho$ describes **monotonic** association between ranked values. Neither establishes causation.

Pearson's coefficient is:

$$
r=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}.
$$

Check: appropriate numerical scoring, an approximately linear relationship if describing it with Pearson, influential outliers, independence between observational pairs, and the assumptions of the chosen confidence interval and significance test. Marginal normality is **not required to compute** $r$; exact conventional small-sample inference has stronger distributional assumptions (notably bivariate normality).

```r
set.seed(123)                                  # Reproducibility
n <- 330                                       # Number of paired observations
CEBQ <- rnorm(n, 3.1, 0.7)                    # Example first subscale
AEBQ <- 1.2 + 0.65 * CEBQ + rnorm(n, 0, 0.6) # Example associated subscale
plot(CEBQ, AEBQ)                              # Examine relationship and outliers
abline(lm(AEBQ ~ CEBQ), col = "blue")         # Add straight-line fit
lines(lowess(CEBQ, AEBQ), col = "red")        # Add flexible smooth curve
cor.test(CEBQ, AEBQ, method = "pearson")      # Pearson estimate, CI, p-value
cor.test(CEBQ, AEBQ, method = "spearman", exact = FALSE) # Rank association
```

**Illustrative results, not code output:** Pearson $r=0.58$, 95% CI $[0.51,0.65]$, $p \lt 0.001$; Spearman $\rho=0.61$, $p \lt 0.001$. Interpret as positive association, not causation.

If Pearson and Spearman disagree, inspect the scatterplot for nonlinear monotonic trends, outliers, ties, and restricted ranges. Do not choose the method because its p-value is smaller. A planned primary analysis may be supplemented with a clearly labelled sensitivity analysis.

**SPSS:** Analyze > Correlate > Bivariate; choose Pearson and/or Spearman. Interpret the seven *corresponding* pairs rather than treating every cell of a large correlation matrix as a planned hypothesis.

### Correlation magnitude

A conventional Evans-style descriptive scale for $|r|$ is: 0.00–0.19 very weak, 0.20–0.39 weak, 0.40–0.59 moderate, 0.60–0.79 strong, and 0.80–1.00 very strong. These boundaries are conventions, **not** universal clinical cut-offs. Report the coefficient, valid $n$, confidence interval and p-value.

With seven planned tests, consider multiplicity adjustment. The chance of at least one false positive among seven *independent* true-null tests at $\alpha=0.05$ is:

$$
1-(1-0.05)^7\approx0.302.
$$

```r
p_values <- c(.001, .008, .021, .038, .046, .120, .310) # Hypothetical p-values
p.adjust(p_values, method = "holm") # Family-wise error control
p.adjust(p_values, method = "BH")   # False-discovery-rate control
```

## 5. Independent-samples t-test: FHS versus non-FHS

An independent-samples t-test assesses whether two independent population means differ:

$$
H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2,\qquad H_1:\mu_1\ne\mu_2.
$$

Check independent observations, appropriate quantitative outcome, severe skewness or influential outliers within each group, and the relevant variance assumptions. The conventional pooled-variance Student t-test assumes equal population variances; **Welch's test does not**. Welch is often a reasonable default, especially if group sizes are very unequal, but was not specifically requested.

For Welch's t-test:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\bar{X}_1-\bar{X}_2)=\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}.
$$

$$
t=\frac{\bar{X}_1-\bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}.
$$

**Worked example from hypothetical summary statistics:**

| Group | $n$ | Mean | SD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| FHS | 43 | 3.50 | 0.80 |
| Non-FHS | 287 | 3.20 | 0.70 |

The mean difference is $3.50-3.20=0.30$. Welch's $\operatorname{SE}\approx0.129$, $t\approx2.33$, degrees of freedom $\approx52.1$, two-sided $p\approx0.024$, and a 95% CI for the mean difference of approximately $[0.04,0.56]$. These are **illustrative calculated results**, not output from the random simulation below.

```r
set.seed(123)                             # Reproducibility
FHS <- rnorm(43, 3.5, 0.8)               # Simulate smaller group
nonFHS <- rnorm(287, 3.2, 0.7)          # Simulate larger group
score <- c(FHS, nonFHS)                  # Combine outcomes
group <- factor(c(rep("FHS", 43), rep("Non-FHS", 287)),
                levels = c("FHS", "Non-FHS")) # Set group order
data <- data.frame(group, score)          # Create analysis dataset
aggregate(score ~ group, data, mean)     # Group means
aggregate(score ~ group, data, sd)       # Group standard deviations
boxplot(score ~ group, data = data)      # Check distributions and outliers
t.test(score ~ group, data = data, var.equal = FALSE) # Welch's test
t.test(score ~ group, data = data, var.equal = TRUE)  # Pooled Student's test
```

**SPSS:** Analyze > Compare Means > Independent-Samples T Test. The output row *Equal variances assumed* corresponds to pooled Student's t-test; *Equal variances not assumed* corresponds to Welch's t-test. Levene's test can be reported when substantively useful but does not need to determine the method through an automatic significance-based switch.

## 6. Effect size: Cohen's d and Hedges' g

A conventional pooled-SD standardised mean difference is:

$$
d=\frac{\bar{X}_1-\bar{X}_2}{s_p},\qquad
s_p=\sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}.
$$

For the hypothetical groups, $s_p\approx0.7135$ and $d\approx0.42$. Benchmarks near $0.20$, $0.50$ and $0.80$ are sometimes labelled small, medium and large, but **do not establish clinical importance**. Clinical importance needs contextual justification. Hedges' $g$ applies a small-sample bias correction. If variances differ materially, think carefully about the choice of standardising SD; always prioritise the raw mean difference and its CI.

```r
# install.packages("effectsize")           # Install once if necessary
library(effectsize)                       # Load effect-size functions
cohens_d(score ~ group, data = data, pooled_sd = TRUE, ci = 0.95) # d and CI
hedges_g(score ~ group, data = data, pooled_sd = TRUE, ci = 0.95) # g and CI
```

## 7. Non-significant p-value with a non-zero effect size

Example: mean difference $=0.18$, Cohen's $d=0.25$, $p=0.13$, 95% CI for the difference $[-0.06,0.42]$. The data do not establish a difference at the chosen significance level, but they also do not establish equivalence. The relatively small FHS group may limit precision. Non-significance alone does **not** prove that the study was underpowered. Interpret the CI and consider a prospective sample-size calculation for future work.

## 8. Reporting

For correlation: report the paired sample size, coefficient, 95% CI, p-value and any prespecified multiplicity adjustment. For group comparisons: report group $n$, mean (SD), mean difference with 95% CI, chosen t-statistic, degrees of freedom, two-sided p-value and a justified effect size. Avoid lengthy table footnotes that equate Cohen's d with clinical significance.

**Example thesis sentence (hypothetical):** "The FHS group (n = 43, M = 3.50, SD = 0.80) had a higher mean score than the non-FHS group (n = 287, M = 3.20, SD = 0.70), with a mean difference of 0.30 (95% CI [0.04, 0.56]); Welch's $t(52.1)=2.33$, $p=0.024$, and Cohen's $d=0.42$." Verify the selected method and the actual estimates before using such wording.

## 9. Final methodological checks

1. Define FHS and non-FHS; confirm the meaning and scoring of all subscales.
2. Confirm whether observations are independent or clustered within families.
3. Inspect paired scatterplots for correlations and group distributions for t-tests.
4. Confirm missing-data handling (valid $n$ varies across subscales).
5. Distinguish association from agreement: correlation alone does not demonstrate interchangeability.
6. Decide whether the seven tests are exploratory or confirmatory and whether multiplicity adjustment is needed.
7. Report estimates and uncertainty rather than treating $p=0.05$ as a measure of importance.


## 10. Questions and answers: applying the methods to CEBQ/AEBQ

The questions below are paraphrased from the appointment topics, rather than presented as verbatim quotations. FHS/non-FHS is retained as a study label because its full meaning has not been confirmed. All numerical examples are hypothetical.

### Q1. Are bell-shaped histograms and skewness/kurtosis between -1 and +1 enough to conclude that the subscales are normally distributed?

**Answer:** They provide reassuring descriptive evidence, but do not prove normality. Check Q–Q plots and unusual observations as well. A significant Shapiro–Wilk test can detect minor deviations with large samples. More importantly, assess the assumptions of the *planned analysis* rather than requiring every questionnaire score to be perfectly normal.

### Q2. If Shapiro–Wilk is significant, must the analysis become non-parametric?

**Answer:** No. Inspect the size and pattern of deviation. For Pearson correlation, examine linearity, influential outliers, independence and inferential assumptions. For a two-group mean comparison, inspect each group's distribution and outliers. Mild non-normality does not automatically invalidate these analyses.

### Q3. Does the Central Limit Theorem mean that a sample larger than 30 is normal? Is N = 326–337 enough to justify parametric tests?

**Answer:** No. The CLT concerns the *sampling distribution of a mean* under suitable conditions, not the distribution of individual scores. A large sample often improves the robustness of mean-based inference, but does not guarantee valid correlation inference or eliminate problems from clustering, outliers or nonlinear relationships.

### Q4. Should Pearson or Spearman be used for correlations between CEBQ and AEBQ subscales?

**Answer:** If the scores are suitable quantitative measures and the scatterplot shows an approximately linear relationship without influential outliers, Pearson is a reasonable choice. Spearman is useful for monotonic but nonlinear relationships, ranked outcomes, or as a sensitivity analysis. The choice should reflect the research question and data, not whichever p-value is smaller.

### Q5. If Pearson and Spearman produce different results, should both be reported?

**Answer:** First investigate the reason: nonlinearity, outliers, ties, or restricted ranges. Report the prespecified primary method and, when informative, a clearly labelled sensitivity analysis. Explain material differences rather than treating significance as the selection criterion.

### Q6. Can Evans' thresholds be used to describe correlation strength? Are they reliable?

**Answer:** They can be used as a *descriptive convention*, provided the source is cited and the labels are not treated as universal scientific boundaries. The actual coefficient and its confidence interval are more informative. For example, correlations of $0.39$ and $0.40$ are practically similar even if categorised differently.

| Absolute correlation | Evans-style descriptive label |
|---|---|
| 0.00–0.19 | Very weak |
| 0.20–0.39 | Weak |
| 0.40–0.59 | Moderate |
| 0.60–0.79 | Strong |
| 0.80–1.00 | Very strong |

### Q7. Which independent-samples t-test p-value should go into Table 3: 'equal variances assumed' or 'equal variances not assumed'?

**Answer:** Report the p-value from the test chosen for the analysis. In standard SPSS output, **equal variances assumed** is the pooled-variance Student's t-test, while **equal variances not assumed** is Welch's t-test. Levene's test assesses evidence of unequal variances, but selecting a row solely from a Levene p-value is not compulsory. Welch's is often a defensible default, especially with unequal group sizes and possible variance differences. Report which test was used consistently, together with the mean difference and 95% CI.

### Q8. Does the smaller FHS group ($n=43$) mean the t-test is invalid or that non-significant findings are due to low power?

**Answer:** Not automatically. Unequal group sizes do not invalidate a two-group comparison. They can reduce precision, particularly if the smaller group is variable. Examine the FHS distribution and outliers, use an appropriate variance assumption, and interpret the confidence interval. A non-significant p-value does not prove that low power caused the result. Future sample-size planning can use a substantively meaningful difference and plausible variability.

### Q9. What does Cohen's d tell us? Does a small, medium or large d indicate clinical significance?

**Answer:** Cohen's $d$ is the difference between two group means expressed in standard-deviation units:

$$
d = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{s_p}
$$

Benchmarks of about $0.20$, $0.50$ and $0.80$ are generic descriptive conventions, **not clinical-importance thresholds**. Clinical relevance requires subject-matter justification, preferably an established meaningful difference on the questionnaire scale. Report the raw mean difference and 95% CI as well.

### Q10. If the t-test is non-significant but Cohen's d is not zero, what should be concluded?

**Answer:** The estimate can be non-zero while its uncertainty includes zero. For example, a hypothetical mean difference of $0.18$ with 95% CI $[-0.06, 0.42]$ is compatible with no difference *and* some positive differences. Do not claim equivalence or absence of effect merely because $p \geq 0.05$.

### Q11. Should Table 3's footnote discuss Cohen's d, the small FHS sample, lack of power and the need for future larger studies?

**Answer:** Keep the **table note** factual and short: define abbreviations, the direction of the mean difference, the statistical test, the confidence interval and the effect-size measure. Put limitations about group imbalance, precision and future sample sizes in the **Discussion**, not a long table note. Do not claim inadequate power without supporting analysis.

**Example table note:** "Values are mean (SD). Mean difference is FHS minus non-FHS. Two-sided Welch's t-tests were used; 95% confidence intervals are shown. Cohen's d is the pooled-SD standardised mean difference." Adapt this to the actual method used.

### Q12. What else should be checked before finalising the analyses?

**Answer:** Confirm the meaning of FHS, scoring rules for CEBQ/AEBQ, whether observations are independent (including any family clustering), why valid sample sizes vary between 326 and 337, and whether multiple testing needs adjustment. These design and reporting issues can matter more than a significant normality test.


### Tutor prompts and follow-up checks

- Ask to see the *actual* Q–Q plots, scatterplots, group descriptives and SPSS output before recommending a final test.
- For Pearson, distinguish the coefficient (which can always be computed for nonconstant numerical pairs) from its conventional p-value/CI assumptions. A curved or outlier-driven relationship can be misleading even at large N.
- For t-tests, normality is considered **within groups**, not by pooling both groups. Independence is a design property, not something Shapiro–Wilk can test.
- With group imbalance, Welch's t-test is generally robust to unequal variances; however, neither Welch nor Student's test automatically solves severe outliers or dependence.
- For correlation strength labels, contextual interpretation and the confidence interval should take precedence over arbitrary categories.
- Do not equate statistical significance, standardised effect magnitude and clinical significance: these answer different questions.
